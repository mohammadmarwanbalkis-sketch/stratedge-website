When does a business actually need a strategy engagement?

When the next decision is large enough that getting it wrong is expensive, and the internal debate has stopped converging. Typically that means a stalled growth curve, several opportunities competing for the same capital, or a plan that has to survive scrutiny from a board, bank or investor.

How is this different from writing a business plan?

A business plan documents intent. A strategy engagement tests it. We start from your performance data and market position, build realistic options, and stress-test each against the capital and capacity you actually have — then the plan is written from what survived.

What if the honest answer is that the plan does not work?

You get told. An adviser whose recommendation always agrees with the brief is not adding anything you could not have produced internally. Where the numbers do not support the ambition, the engagement says so and works on what would have to change for them to.

What the work covers

Strategy engagements begin with the uncomfortable part: establishing what is actually true about the business today. Reported revenue rarely tells the whole story, and the difference between a business that is growing and one that is simply getting busier usually shows up in the unit economics rather than the top line.

From that baseline the work moves through four questions in order. Where should the business compete — which segments, which geographies, which customer types actually reward what you do well? How does it win there — what is the basis of preference, and is it defensible? What has to be true for the plan to work — the assumptions that carry the most risk, made explicit and tested. And what is the sequence — because most strategies fail not because the destination was wrong but because the order of moves exceeded the organisation's capacity to absorb them.

The output is a direction with the reasoning shown. You receive the logic, the data and the assumptions behind the recommendation, not only the conclusion — which is what makes it defensible when someone senior challenges it.

Where growth strategies usually go wrong

Three patterns account for most of the failures we are asked to repair.

The plan was never resourced. A strategy that requires capability the business does not have, and has no plan to acquire, is a wish. Realistic planning starts from current capacity and treats capability building as part of the sequence rather than an assumption.

Everything was a priority. When a plan contains eleven initiatives, the organisation quietly chooses its own three, and rarely the three that mattered. Sequencing is not administrative detail; it is most of the strategy.

Nobody owned it after the presentation. Strategy fails at implementation more often than at design. Engagements close with named owners, milestones and a measurement framework precisely because this is where the value is usually lost.

How the engagement runs

The same four-phase method applies whether the work runs for two weeks or six months — only the depth changes. Diagnose establishes the baseline. Design builds and tests the options. Deliver converts the decision into owners, milestones and measures. Sustain installs the reporting that shows whether it is working, and hands the model over to your team.
